15.1 · What Is Waste? The Waste Stream

Waste, MSW, and the Waste Stream

  • Waste or garbage is what we determine to be unusable or unwanted
  • Municipal solid waste (MSW): the ~4.9 lbs/person/day Americans add to the waste stream from households and businesses, nearly double the 1960 level.
  • Solid waste: all discarded solid, liquid, semisolid, or gaseous material.
  • Upstream processes generate far more waste: producing 30 microchips generates 90 lbs of waste before the 1.5 oz chip enters your hands.
  • Life cycle assessment accounts for environmental impacts of all steps.

15.3 · Incineration and Conversion

Burning and Converting Waste

🔥Incineration
  • Controlled high-temperature combustion with pollution controls reduce waste volume by 80–90%.
  • Mass burn: combusts solid waste first, then gases secondarily. Refuse-derived fuel removes non-combustibles first, improving efficiency.
  • Air pollution remains a concern and requires careful emissions monitoring.
♻Conversion
  • Waste-to-energy: incineration heat powers steam turbines generating electricity.
  • Bioreactor landfill: injected water and air accelerate decomposition, reducing waste volume.
  • Remediation converts hazardous waste to less hazardous forms: bioremediation (microorganisms), mycoremediation (fungi), phytoremediation (plants).
  • Landfill methane can be filtered and burned for electricity or converted to vehicle fuel.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.

15.4 · Recycling: Types, Economics, and Limitations

Primary, Secondary, and the Market

Recycling is a strategy of redefining “waste” as “resources” for new products, thus diverting materials from the waste stream.

US recycling rate grew from 6% in 1960 to 34% in 2018.

Primary Recycling
Converts waste into the same type of product (aluminum cans → cans). Most efficient closed-loop approach.
Secondary Recycling
Converts waste into a different product (bottles → fleece). Often called 'downcycling,' it requires more energy and reduces quality.
Design for Recovery
Standards for manufacturing materials to facilitate efficient recycling. Consumer behavior, collection systems, and product design all play roles.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
15.4 · Advantages and Limitations of Recycling

What Recycling Can and Cannot Do

  • Recycling reduces landfill and incineration impacts, saves energy, and cuts GHG emissions compared to producing goods from virgin materials.
  • The market for recyclables fluctuates to the point where some recycled materials end up in landfills.
  • Recycling alone cannot solve the waste problem; reduction and reuse must come first.

15.5 · Culture, Consumption, and the Post-War Shift

From Frugality to the Consumer Age

  • Pre-1950s: newspapers and magazines defined household success by how little was wasted; cloth sacks were reused as clothing, food scraps recycled.
  • Post-WWII prosperity: growing wealth, advancing technology, and an increasing array of consumer goods shifted culture toward purchasing and disposal.
  • Conspicuous consumption: buying goods to project particular identities within society, designer brands, larger homes, more cars.

15.6 · What Can We Learn From Our Garbage?

The Archaeology of Us: Garbology

  • 'Garbology,' initiated by William Rathje at the University of Arizona, treats landfills as archaeological sites, revealing what we actually throw away vs. what we say we throw away.
  • Garbology has contributed to understanding what we recycle, how to improve recyclable products, and how much waste we generate.
  • Analysis of landfill contents has improved recyclable product design and helped cities understand where waste reduction programs are most needed.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
15.6 · The Waste Reduction Hierarchy

Not All Strategies Are Equal

1
Reduce: Consume less in the first place: the most effective and least resource-intensive strategy.
2
Reuse: Extend the useful life of goods through repair, sharing, and repurposing before they become waste.
3
Recycle: Process waste materials into new products, which is effective but still requires energy and resources.
4
Recover: Extract energy from waste through incineration or landfill gas capture, which still produces residual waste.
5
Dispose: Landfilling as a last resort after all other options have been exhausted.
